Setting up a clinic or day procedure centre in Hong Kong

A working guide for mainland and overseas investors: what Cap. 633 requires, who can own and run a facility, how long licensing takes, and where projects usually stall. Last reviewed September 2026. Back to the Greater Bay Area hub.

Why investors choose Hong Kong

Hong Kong is a small market with an outsized role: a common-law jurisdiction with free capital movement, an internationally trained medical profession, and a government that has set out to make the city an international medical innovation hub. Four developments since 2023 matter most for a new facility:

  • A clear licensing code. The clinic and small-practice-clinic regime under the Private Healthcare Facilities Ordinance commenced on 13 October 2025 with a published Code of Practice for Clinics, following the hospital (2019) and day procedure centre (2020) regimes. Requirements are written down and inspected against, which makes planning predictable.
  • Faster access to new medicines. The "1+" mechanism (November 2023, extended to all new drugs in November 2024) lets a drug be registered on one reference-authority approval plus local data; more than 20 new drugs have been approved this way. Phase 1 of Hong Kong's own "primary evaluation" started on 31 March 2026, a priority review pathway for Hospital Authority-recommended innovative drugs followed in June 2026, and the Hong Kong Centre for Medical Products Regulation is due to be established by the end of 2026.
  • Cross-border demand. Hong Kong patients are travelling to Shenzhen, and Greater Bay Area patients are coming to Hong Kong for specialist care. Schemes such as the Elderly Health Care Voucher pilot and the Hospital Authority's GBA patient pilot (extended to March 2027) are institutionalising those flows.
  • Private capacity is policy. The Government has said it regards the private sector as an essential part of the system and is legislating for private fee transparency while expanding the public system's purchasing of private services. Well-run private facilities have a role in that plan.

Licence categories under Cap. 633

The Ordinance regulates four types of private healthcare facility where registered medical practitioners or dentists practise. Choosing the right category is the first decision, because it fixes the premises, staffing and governance standards you will be inspected against.

For an investor-owned facility employing doctors, the relevant categories are the clinic licence and, where scheduled procedures are planned, the DPC licence. Facilities practised in only by Chinese medicine practitioners or allied health professionals fall outside Cap. 633 and are regulated under their own professional legislation.

Ownership, governance and the Chief Medical Executive

The licensee may be an individual, a partnership or a company; there is no nationality or residency restriction on shareholders. The Ordinance instead places accountability on named people inside the facility:

  • Chief Medical Executive (CME). Every clinic must at all times have a CME who is a medical practitioner (or, for a dental-only facility, a dentist) registered in Hong Kong for not less than four years, and who is accountable for the clinical management of the facility. DPCs and hospitals carry their own CME and specialty-adviser requirements.
  • Policies and records. The Code of Practice expects written policies on patient records, consent, incident reporting, complaints, medication management, infection control and emergencies, and expects them to be followed and auditable.
  • Naming. Section 92 and Schedule 8 restrict terms such as "hospital", "clinic" and "medical centre" to licensed or exempted facilities. The Government intends to bring these provisions into force with the unlicensed-operation penalties, targeted for the end of 2027, and has proposed adding terms such as 臨床, 醫務 and 診治 to the schedule.

Premises and healthcare engineering

Premises are where most first-time applications lose months. Beyond floor area and layout, the Code of Practice and the Department of Health's inspection checklists look at ventilation and air-change rates for treatment rooms, medical gas and suction for DPCs, dirty-to-clean workflow, sterilisation and storage, clinical waste, accessibility and fire safety. In Hong Kong's commercial buildings the practical constraints are often lease permissions, base-building ventilation and the cost of adding dedicated exhaust — so we review the site before the lease is signed, not after fit-out.

Doctors, nurses and registration

All doctors practising in the facility must be registered with the Medical Council of Hong Kong; nurses with the Nursing Council; allied health professionals under the Supplementary Medical Professions Ordinance where applicable. The 2021 amendment to the Medical Registration Ordinance created a special registration pathway for non-locally trained doctors, but it is tied to service in public institutions before full registration, so a private clinic generally plans around fully registered practitioners. Building the clinical team — including the four-year-registered CME — is therefore a critical-path item alongside the premises.

Timeline and process

  1. Feasibility (2–4 weeks). Licence category, clinical scope, site screening, staffing plan, indicative capital and operating requirements.
  2. Design and documentation (6–12 weeks, in parallel with fit-out). Layout and engineering review, equipment schedule, policies and SOPs, staff credentials and training records.
  3. Application. The Department of Health asks new facilities to apply at least two months before the intended opening. IHI files as your authorised representative and handles the regulator's queries.
  4. Inspection. When documents are complete the Department typically schedules an inspection within about ten days. We run a mock inspection first and attend the real one.
  5. Decision and opening. Results are issued within fifteen working days of the inspection, subject to any follow-up actions. Clinic licences run for five years and DPC licences for three, with renewal and variation applications thereafter.
Applications have been accepted through the Department of Health's e-Licensing system since October 2025. As at 1 June 2026 the Department had received 813 clinic licence applications and issued 118 licences, alongside 2,937 letters of exemption for small practice clinics — a useful benchmark for the throughput you can expect.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a Hong Kong-registered doctor to open a clinic?

Yes. Every clinic must have a Chief Medical Executive registered in Hong Kong for at least four years, and all doctors practising there must be registered with the Medical Council. The owner of the licensee company does not need to be a doctor.

Can doctors trained outside Hong Kong work in my private clinic?

Only if they hold Hong Kong registration. The 2021 special registration pathway for non-locally trained doctors runs through public institutions before full registration, so private facilities generally rely on fully registered practitioners.

How long does a Hong Kong clinic licence take?

Apply at least two months before opening. With complete documents, inspection is typically scheduled within about ten days and the decision issued within fifteen working days of the inspection. Premises readiness is the usual reason timelines slip.

Are there restrictions on calling my business a clinic or medical centre?

Yes — section 92 and Schedule 8 of Cap. 633 restrict such terms to licensed or exempted facilities. The Government intends to bring them into force with the unlicensed-operation penalties, targeted for the end of 2027.

Can I open a Chinese medicine clinic under the same licence?

Premises practised in only by Chinese medicine practitioners are outside Cap. 633 and are regulated under the Chinese Medicine Ordinance. A facility combining registered medical practitioners and Chinese medicine practitioners needs the Cap. 633 licence for the medical practice and must meet the professional rules for both.
